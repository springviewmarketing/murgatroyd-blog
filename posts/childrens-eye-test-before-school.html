17 August 2026 · Children's eyecare

Does my child need an eye test before they go back to school?

Children almost never tell you their sight is blurred. They assume everybody else sees the world exactly as they do, so there is nothing to report. That is why booking an eye test before term starts is one of the more useful things you can do in August.

How do I know if my child needs an eye test?

In most cases you cannot know from the outside, which is why the honest answer is that every child benefits from a proper eye examination whether or not anything seems wrong. A child who has never seen clearly has no reference point for what clear looks like. They will not complain, they will simply adapt, and the adaptations tend to be mistaken for something else. A child who cannot see the board comfortably drifts off during lessons. A child whose eyes struggle to work together avoids reading because it makes their head ache. A child with a difference in prescription between the two eyes can pass a quick vision check with flying colours because the stronger eye quietly does the work of both.

This is the part that surprises parents most. Good school reports and confident behaviour do not rule out an uncorrected prescription. Nor does the absence of squinting or headaches. An eye examination is a measurement, not an interview, and it picks up things that no amount of watching your child at home will reveal.

Isn't the school vision screening enough?

Screening is genuinely useful, but it is not the same thing as an eye examination and it was never designed to be. The UK National Screening Committee recommends vision screening for children aged four to five, and local authorities are responsible for delivering it as part of the Healthy Child Programme, which is why most children are offered it during their Reception year.

The purpose of that screening is narrow and deliberate. It is looking for reduced distance vision in one or both eyes so that children who need a full assessment can be identified and referred. It is not checking the health of the eye, it is not measuring how the two eyes work together at reading distance, and it does not produce a prescription. A child can pass screening comfortably and still be long sighted enough that sustained close work is uncomfortable, because young eyes are very good at compensating for long sight over short periods.

There is also a timing problem. Screening happens once, in Reception, so a prescription that develops at eight or nine, a very common age for short sight to appear, sits entirely outside that net. Children who move between areas or who were away on the day can miss the session altogether.

At what age should a child have their first eye test?

There is no minimum age, and no lower limit at which we would turn a child away. As a general rule, if nothing has concerned you, somewhere around the age of three is a sensible first appointment, and certainly before starting school. If something has concerned you, bring them at any age, including infancy.

Come sooner if there is a family history of squint, lazy eye or a strong prescription, since these run in families and early detection genuinely changes what can be done about them. Come sooner if you have noticed an eye turning inwards or outwards, even occasionally, or if your child was born prematurely. In these cases, waiting for the school screening is the wrong call, because some conditions respond far better to treatment when they are picked up young.

Does my child need to be able to read or talk?

No. This is easily the most common reason parents delay a first appointment, and it is based on a misunderstanding of what the test involves. A great deal of an eye examination is objective, meaning the optometrist measures how the eye focuses light directly rather than asking the child anything at all.

For a child who is not yet reading, letters are replaced with pictures, shapes or matching cards that the child can point to or hand over. For a child who is not yet talking, we watch how the eyes fixate and follow, and we use a technique called retinoscopy, in which a light is shone into the eye and lenses are moved in front of it while the optometrist observes the reflection from the back of the eye. That reflection tells us the prescription without a single word from the child. It works on babies, and on children having a difficult day, which is why a toddler can leave with an accurate prescription.

What actually happens in a children's eye test?

It is quieter and less clinical than most parents expect, and it is usually over in about half an hour. The appointment starts with a conversation rather than equipment, covering general health, any family history of eye conditions, how your child is getting on at school, and anything you have noticed at home. That history shapes everything that follows, so it is worth mentioning small things even if they feel trivial.

The optometrist then measures how clearly each eye sees, separately and together, using whichever chart suits your child's stage. Retinoscopy and lens work establish the prescription. Separate checks look at how well the eyes move, how well they turn inwards together for close work, and whether the brain is comfortably combining the two images into one, which is the part that most affects reading stamina. Finally the optometrist examines the health of the eyes themselves, front and back, using a bright light and magnification.

In some cases we may suggest eye drops that temporarily relax the focusing muscle, which allows a more accurate measurement in children whose focusing is very active. This is not routine, and it is always discussed with you first rather than sprung on you.

What signs should I look for at home?

The useful signs are behavioural rather than visual, because a child will change what they do long before they say anything. Sitting unusually close to the television, holding books or a tablet very close, or moving nearer to the board at school all suggest that distance vision is doing something it should not have to.

Screwing up the eyes to look at something far away is a genuine indicator, since narrowing the eyelids temporarily sharpens a blurred image. So is a persistent head tilt or turn, which can be a way of managing a squint or a difference between the eyes. Covering or closing one eye to read, frequent eye rubbing that is not related to tiredness or hay fever, and one eye drifting while the other stays fixed are all worth an appointment.

Then there is the group of signs that tend to get attributed to other causes. Headaches that arrive in the afternoon and at the end of the school week. Losing place while reading, skipping lines, or still using a finger to track long after other children have stopped. Avoiding reading and homework while happily doing everything else. None of these prove there is a problem with the eyes, but all of them justify ruling it out before assuming the cause is attitude or ability.

Is a children's eye test free on the NHS?

Yes. The NHS funds sight tests for anyone under sixteen, and for those aged sixteen, seventeen and eighteen who are in full time education, which includes being taught at school, college, university or at home. There is no charge to you for the examination itself.

If glasses are needed, there is help with those too. Children under sixteen, and sixteen to eighteen year olds in full time education, are entitled to an NHS optical voucher towards the cost of spectacles or contact lenses. The value of the voucher depends on the strength and type of lenses required, and it can be put towards a wider choice of frames, with any difference payable by you. We will always tell you clearly what the voucher covers before you choose anything, so there are no surprises at the counter.

What happens if my child does need glasses?

Very little drama, in our experience. Children accept glasses far more easily than their parents expect, largely because the improvement is immediate and obvious to them in a way it rarely is for an adult. The bigger practical question is not whether they will wear them but how the glasses are set up.

A children's frame has to fit properly at the bridge and behind the ear, sit at the right height so that your child looks through the middle of the lens rather than over the top, and survive being taken off and put down in ways that no adult frame ever endures. That is a fitting job rather than a shopping job, and it is the part worth taking time over. We will also tell you when the glasses are needed, since some prescriptions are for full time wear and others only for reading, board work or screen use, and being clear about that from the start avoids a lot of arguments at home.

Expect the fit to need adjusting through the year as your child grows and as the frames take their inevitable knocks. It matters more than people realise, because glasses that have slipped down the nose are not delivering the prescription they were made for.

What is myopia management, and would my child need it?

Myopia, or short sight, is the prescription that makes distant objects blurred while close work stays clear. It usually appears during the school years and tends to progress as the eye continues to grow, which is why a child's prescription often increases at each visit for several years before settling.

Myopia management is the term for treatments designed to slow that progression rather than simply correct the blur. The options include specially designed spectacle lenses, certain contact lenses worn during the day, lenses worn overnight, and prescription eye drops. They are not suitable or necessary for every short sighted child, and the right choice depends on the child's age, prescription, how quickly it is changing, family history and how they get on with lenses in practice. If your child is becoming short sighted, this is a conversation worth having early rather than after several years of increases, and you can read more about our approach on the Murgatroyd Opticians myopia management page.

How often should my child's eyes be tested?

For a child with no identified problems, every two years is the general NHS guidance, and many practices review children more frequently than that because prescriptions can change quickly during growth. The important point is that these intervals are a starting position rather than a rule.

The College of Optometrists suggests considerably shorter intervals where there is something to monitor. For children under seven with a binocular vision anomaly or a refractive error, and for those aged seven to fifteen with a binocular vision anomaly or rapidly progressing short sight, it suggests reviewing at six months. The College is also explicit that the intervals should not be applied automatically to every patient in a category, and that clinical judgement about the individual child comes first. In practice that means your optometrist will tell you when to come back, and that recommendation is based on your child specifically rather than on a standard schedule.

Frequently asked questions

Can my child have an eye test if they are too young to talk?

Yes. Objective techniques such as retinoscopy measure the prescription by observing how light reflects from the back of the eye, so no response from your child is required. Eye alignment and movement are assessed by watching how the eyes behave, using lights and targets rather than questions.

My child passed the school screening. Should I still book an eye test?

Yes, if it has been a while or if anything has changed. Screening checks distance vision at one moment in Reception year. It does not assess eye health, focusing at reading distance, or how the two eyes work together, and it cannot account for a prescription that develops later in primary school.

Do I need a referral from the GP or the school?

No. You can book an NHS funded sight test for your child directly with us at either practice. If we find something that needs a hospital eye service opinion, we will arrange that referral ourselves and explain what happens next.

Will my child definitely need drops in their eyes?

No, and most children do not. Drops that relax the focusing muscle are used selectively, when a child's focusing is active enough to affect the accuracy of the measurement. If we think they would be helpful we will explain why, and we will usually arrange a separate appointment so that you can plan around the few hours of blurred near vision afterwards.

Can screen time damage my child's eyes?

Long screen sessions are far more likely to cause temporary discomfort than lasting damage. Sustained close focusing and a reduced blink rate leave eyes feeling tired, dry and gritty, and headaches often follow. Regular breaks away from close work help, as does making sure that any prescription for close work is up to date, because an uncorrected eye works harder for the same result.

What should I bring to the appointment?

Bring any current glasses, even if they are broken or rarely worn, along with details of previous prescriptions if you have them. It also helps to bring a note of anything the school has mentioned and any relevant family history of eye conditions. If your child has a favourite toy that helps them settle, bring that too.

How long does a children's appointment take?

Allow around half an hour. Younger children sometimes need a little longer, and we would rather take the extra few minutes than rush a child who needs a moment to get comfortable with what is happening.
