Julia works on reception at our Staveley practice on High Street. She has spent around 35 years in optics, including home-visit eye care and frame fitting and repairs, and joined us three years ago. Aileen first met her more than 25 years ago, when they worked together in Rotherham, and they've been friends ever since. This is her story, told with her permission.

## It started with an ordinary eye test

In March 2024, Julia had an eye test with Aileen at our Staveley practice. She knew her sight had been slipping. "It comes on gradually," she says. "You don't realise until you get to the point where you think, what's going on here?"

Aileen found a cataract and referred her for surgery. At the consultation, Julia asked what her vision would be like once the cataract was out. The answer surprised her: still blurred, because she had a second condition in the same eye, one she had never heard of. It was Fuchs' endothelial dystrophy.

The first clinic could remove a cataract but couldn't do a corneal transplant, and a second referral hit the same wall. Julia saw no point in having one operation without the other. Aileen and Phil, drawing on their experience with other patients, referred her to the Royal Hallamshire in Sheffield for an opinion on both conditions together.

## What Fuchs' dystrophy does to the cornea

The cornea is the clear window at the front of your eye. Its innermost layer is a sheet of cells that keep it clear by pumping fluid out. In Fuchs' dystrophy, those cells fail over time, fluid builds up, and the cornea swells and clouds.

[Moorfields Eye Hospital's guide to Fuchs' dystrophy](https://www.moorfields.nhs.uk/eye-conditions/fuchs-dystrophy) describes the classic early sign as misty vision on waking that clears as the day goes on. As the condition progresses, the blur lasts all day. It affects both eyes and is more common in women than men, and symptoms tend to appear in your 50s or later. If your sight is foggy first thing and better by lunchtime, mention it at your next eye examination.

Cataract surgery can make Fuchs' worse, so surgeons plan the two operations together, as Julia's did.

## Two operations, in the right order

In Sheffield, a corneal consultant advised removing the cataract first, then carrying out the transplant around six to twelve weeks later. "In an ideal world it would be six weeks," Julia remembers him saying. "In reality, it's more like twelve."

Her cataract came out on 8 May at the Northern General Hospital. The Royal Hallamshire booked her transplant for 5 August, then brought it forward to 15 July. The surgeon replaced the two innermost layers of her cornea with a thin disc of donor tissue.

From that first eye test to the transplant took more than two years. "It's been quite a long wait," Julia says. "But I'm there."

## Three days looking at the ceiling

After this kind of transplant, the surgeon leaves a bubble of air or gas inside the eye to press the new layer into place. The bubble floats upwards, so lying face up keeps it pressed against the graft. Julia's instructions were three days on her back, with a 15-minute break in every hour for a drink or the toilet. Her team told her the more she stuck to it, the better her chances.

She spent the first day flat on the sofa and couldn't sleep that night for backache. On day two she changed tactics: feet up on the sofa, a travel pillow round her neck, and her head tipped back so her face still pointed at the ceiling.

## The glasses David found

When David heard what Julia was facing, he sourced her a pair of recumbent prism glasses. Each lens holds a prism that turns your line of sight through 90 degrees. Lie flat, look straight up, and you see what's in front of your feet, the television included, the right way up.

"You just think that can't work," Julia says. "But it does."

They worked so well that she lost track of her own posture. "After a few hours you forget," she says. "I'd be thinking, am I still posturing? Am I still looking up?" More than once she took them off to check.

Prism glasses have no prescription of their own, and some styles fit over your normal glasses.

## The day the colour came back

The day after surgery, Julia could see shapes and count the fingers her surgeon held up. The following morning, everything had gone. "Totally grey," she says. "No shapes, no colour, nothing."

On the evening of 3 August, watching television, she covered her left eye to test the right. Looking straight ahead, she still saw grey, but out of the side she could make out the corner of the room. The next morning she covered her left eye again. "I could see everything. The colour had come back."

The hospital told her fluid had built up at the back of the new cornea and had since drained. [Leeds Teaching Hospitals' corneal graft aftercare advice](https://www.leedsth.nhs.uk/patients/resources/corneal-graft-post-operative-advice/) warns transplant patients that vision can get better and worse before it settles, and can take anywhere from a few weeks to over a year. If your sight drops after eye surgery, contact the hospital that did the operation straight away, as Julia did.

## A gift from a stranger

Julia's consultant gave her two magnified images of the donor tissue and told her the one thing he could: it came from a woman aged 56. "She's given me my sight back," Julia says.

[NHS Blood and Transplant reports](https://www.organdonation.nhs.uk/get-involved/news/thousands-of-people-are-needlessly-living-with-sight-loss-because-of-a-shortage-of-donors/) 5,071 cornea transplants in the UK in 2024/25, while the number of cornea donors fell by 3% to 4,976. It estimates more than 4,000 people in England are living with sight loss that a transplant could restore. Poor eyesight, including cataracts, doesn't stop you donating. England runs an opt-out system, but your family is always involved in the decision, so tell them what you want. You can [record your organ and tissue donation decision on the NHS Organ Donor Register](https://www.organdonation.nhs.uk/).

## What happens next

Julia is still under the hospital and still on steroid eye drops, reducing them step by step. She expects to be on them until around the end of the year. Her sight is good again, but the eye still has some astigmatism, so she'll need new glasses. She'll wait until her consultant confirms the eye has settled, because the cornea keeps changing shape as it heals. Leeds Teaching Hospitals gives transplant patients the same advice: no new spectacles until the eye is stable.

Once the hospital gives her the go-ahead, she'll have a full eye examination with Aileen or Phil. After 35 years of helping other people choose frames, she'll be on the other side of the counter again.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long do you have to lie flat after a corneal transplant?

It varies by hospital and by type of graft, from one to two hours at [Moorfields](https://www.moorfields.nhs.uk/for-patients/information-hub/endothelial-keratoplasty) to four to seven days at the [Royal Free](https://www.royalfree.nhs.uk/patients-and-visitors/patient-information-leaflets/having-a-dmek-or-dsaek-corneal-transplant). Julia's instructions were three days, with a 15-minute break every hour. Follow your own hospital's leaflet.

### Can you watch television while lying flat after eye surgery?

Yes, with prism glasses. They turn your view through 90 degrees, so you can keep your face pointed at the ceiling and still see the screen. Check with your hospital team that screen time is fine for you.

### What are the early signs of Fuchs' dystrophy?

Misty or blurred vision when you wake up that clears during the day is the classic early sign. Later, the blur lasts all day. An optometrist can pick it up at an eye examination, so mention any morning blur at your next test.

### Why would you have cataract surgery before a corneal transplant?

Cataract surgery can make Fuchs' dystrophy worse, so surgeons plan the two together. Some do both in one operation. Julia's surgeons removed the cataract first and carried out the transplant about ten weeks later.

### Is it normal for vision to get worse after a corneal transplant?

Vision can fluctuate before it settles, and full recovery can take months or longer. Julia's sight went grey two days after surgery and came back nearly three weeks later. Report a sudden drop in vision to the hospital that did your operation.

### When can I get new glasses after a corneal transplant?

Only once your surgeon confirms the eye has settled. The cornea changes shape as it heals, so a prescription taken too early can soon be out of date.

### Can I donate my corneas if I wear glasses or have had cataracts?

Yes. NHS Blood and Transplant confirms that poor eyesight, including cataracts, doesn't stop you being a cornea donor.
